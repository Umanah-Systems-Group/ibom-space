Agbára, Igwe and Caelum

One servicing tug, the iodine engine inside it, and the communications parts that link them. All three are designed and simulated in Easley, our own engineering platform, and all three are designs today.

Film: a satellite in orbit

Grab it by the ring. Move it anywhere.

Almost every satellite bigger than a CubeSat still carries the ring it was launched on. Agbára, our servicing tug, flies up to it, clamps onto that ring, and becomes the satellite’s engine. Then it lets go and flies to the next one.

Agbára is a design today; the film shows other spacecraft, not Ibom hardware. We aim for ground tests in 2027 and a flight demonstration after that.

Find the customer

Agbára rides up as a rideshare payload. Its main tank holds iodine as a solid, so it launches unpressurised. Igwe spirals it up to the customer’s orbit and parks it behind, in the same plane.

From about 1.2 km, Anya’s rendezvous lidar locks on and ranges the customer to within centimetres.

Close in, slowly

Inside the last kilometre the Hall thruster switches off so its plume never touches the customer. Small cold-gas pods do the fine moves.

Jagora, Agbára’s guidance and control, holds at set distances for a go from the ground. From 35 m the lidar switches to 3-D imaging, finds the ring and checks the customer’s spin. Every trajectory drifts safely away if the tug goes quiet.

Capture

At a few centimetres a second, Agbára touches the ring. Jide, the capture system, has sensors on three posts, set 120° apart, trip and the clamp closes. The clamp tolerates 25 mm of misplacement either way, and a motor brake locks it shut.

Every approach and capture command is authenticated and every docking decision is signed and logged, through our sister company Vigilantyx.

Become its engine

Small test burns measure the combined mass. A gimbal steers the Igwe thruster to push straight through the pair’s centre of mass, and the tug fires in sunlight, on solar power, for days or weeks.

The same push serves three jobs: dispose of a satellite, move it to a new orbit, or keep it on station after its own fuel runs out.

Let go

The clamp opens and the cold-gas pods back the tug away along a safe corridor. For a disposal, the tug lets go low enough that air drag finishes the descent, and the old satellite burns up.

On to the next one

Constellations put many satellites in each orbital plane, so one tug can serve several customers in turn. When its iodine and cold gas run low, it disposes of itself.

See how Agbára navigates

Anya, how Agbára sees

Anya, Agbára’s eyes, navigates to the one thing the tug grabs: the customer’s launch ring. It has to know where that ring is, how fast the gap is closing and which way the satellite is turned, with no help from the satellite. Lidar and cameras on board answer that many times a second.

  1. Lidar

    A rendezvous lidar ranges the customer from about 1.2 km, then measures its full 3-D pose from 35 m down to 1.6 m, in sunlight or darkness.

  2. Cameras

    Narrow and wide stereo cameras, a thermal camera and an LED light for eclipse cover the far approach and the last 1.6 m, where the lidar is too close to see. There, close-range vision software adds a second pose.

  3. Two answers must agree

    One solver fits the launch ring’s ellipse in the camera image; another matches the satellite’s 3-D shape in the lidar data. If they disagree, Jagora holds or backs off.

  4. Simulated in Easley

    Approaches are flown in Easley’s simulation hundreds of times at a stretch, with random lighting and injected sensor failures. In the runs so far the tug caught every failure and never touched the customer unsafely. Lab tests come later.

Four jobs, one machine

Disposal

A dead or retiring satellite is pushed down to burn up, so its operator meets the five-year deorbit rule.

For constellation operators and defence agencies with hundreds of satellites in low orbit.

Relocation

A satellite is raised, lowered or moved to a new slot, without having been designed to do it.

For operators and for defence customers asking for manoeuvre on demand.

Life extension

The tug stays attached and does the station keeping after the satellite’s own fuel runs out.

For geostationary operators, with a larger tug, later.

Inspection

Nyoka flies Agbára around a satellite and images it with lidar and cameras, before any capture or as a service on its own.

For operators checking damage or a failed deployment, and for agencies watching what is in orbit.

Buyers are already paying for this work: the US Space Development Agency bought its first satellite disposal service in January 2026. Our edge is the stack: an iodine engine, a launch-ring clamp and rendezvous lidar in one tug, designed in Easley first.

Igwe iodine propulsion

An electric Hall thruster that runs on iodine, sold as parts to any spacecraft maker. It is also the engine inside our tug.

Iodine sits in the tank as a solid. It needs no high-pressure tank, it can wait fuelled on the ground for years, and it packs far more propellant into the same volume than xenon. Every part is designed, simulated and checked in Easley before it is built.

Hall thruster
Small-spacecraft class, with its own anode manifold and propellant distributor
Power processing unit
Modular, so it scales with the power on board
Iodine tank and feed
Heated tank that turns solid iodine into gas and feeds it in any orientation
Thruster gimbal
360° rotation and 12° tilt

Caelum communications

Radios, laser heads and antennas, sold as parts. Built for the links a gimbal makes awkward: between a tug and its customer, across a formation, and inside a cluster.

Multi-band radio
One tunable synthesizer covers several bands, and several bands can ride over one optical link.
Gimbal-free laser head
Steers its beam with laser arrays instead of moving parts, for short-range optical links.
Rugged and deployable antennas
An all-metal X-band patch, and self-deploying helical antennas that pack flat for launch.

Caelum, the proof in orbit

A showcase spacecraft that flies Igwe and Caelum together, so customers can watch both work before they buy. It is built on a bought bus and is not for sale. It may also be the first friendly target our tug docks with.
